\begin{abstract}
Starlink and OneWeb now serve users in the same Ku band from thousands of
low-Earth-orbit satellites. When their satellites line up in a user's view, the
FCC's rule 47 CFR~25.261 decides who gives way: an in-line event triggers band
splitting, and a later-licensed system must keep the throughput and availability
losses it causes an earlier-licensed system below fixed limits. Whether these
protections hold in practice has not been quantified. We develop a general model
of co-frequency non-geostationary interference and of the rule's criteria, apply
it to both constellations as filed and as deployed, and derive a closed-form
approximation that links the regulatory power criterion to satellite geometry.
The rule is triggered far more often than geometric in-line screens suggest, and
its burden falls mostly on OneWeb, the system it protects, which is affected
three to four times as often as Starlink. Without satellite diversity, Starlink
at its filed maximum power violates both protection limits. Diversity restores
compliance, but only when the victim can switch to another satellite, and
OneWeb's own filed elevation mask often leaves none. The rule's protection
therefore depends on satellite diversity and on the victim's design, neither of
which the rule mentions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Ku-band sky over a user terminal is now crowded: our snapshot counts
\NStarlinkOp\ operational Starlink and \NOnewebOp\ OneWeb satellites, all
transmitting to users in the same band. Continually, somewhere on Earth, a
satellite of one system slides close to the line of sight between a user and a
satellite of the other system. The two signals collide, and someone has to give
way.

In the United States, who gives way is written into 47 CFR~25.261~\cite{cfr25261},
the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rule for sharing spectrum among
non-geostationary-orbit (NGSO) satellite systems. The FCC licenses such systems
in batches called \emph{processing rounds}; a system licensed in an earlier round
is the \emph{incumbent} and one licensed in a later round is the \emph{entrant}.
Two systems are \emph{co-frequency} when they are authorized to use the same
frequencies for the same type of link. The rule makes a simple promise. When interference raises a receiver's noise
temperature by more than \DttThresholdPct\% ($\Delta T/T$), an \emph{in-line
event} occurs and, absent coordination, systems of the same processing round
split the band for as long as it lasts (\S\,25.261(c))~\cite{fcc2329}. Systems of
a later round must do more: under \S\,25.261(d) they may cost an earlier-round
system no more than \DegradeLimitPct\% of its time-averaged throughput and no
more than \UnavailLimitPct\% of its availability, on every link with at least
\RuleBaselineAvailPct\% baseline availability (the fraction of time the link
works without interference) at a carrier-to-noise ratio (C/N) of
\CnGateDb~dB~\cite{fcc24117}. The protection runs for ten years after the first
grant in the later round, after which (c) applies across rounds. OneWeb~\cite{fcc1777}
and the first generation of Starlink (Gen1)~\cite{fcc1838} were licensed in the 2016 round and its second
generation (Gen2)~\cite{fcc2291} in the 2020 round, so the rule promises OneWeb protection
against Starlink Gen2. A separate 2026 order rewrote sharing between NGSO and geostationary (GSO)
systems~\cite{fcc2626}
but left these NGSO--NGSO criteria unchanged.

Whether this promise is kept in practice has not been measured. How often does
the event fire? Where on Earth? Which system absorbs it? And when a
later-round system meets the limits, is it the rule that protects the incumbent,
or something the incumbent does itself? The answers are not obvious, because the
interference geometry changes every second, operators steer and power-control
their beams, and the victim's antenna sees interferers through its sidelobes, not
only along the line of sight. Interference between the OneWeb and SpaceX user
downlinks was anticipated early~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{delportillo2019}; later work asked
whether interference among dense NGSO constellations matters in
practice~\cite{braun2019} and how satellite selection enables in-band
coexistence~\cite{kim2025}. None of these evaluates the FCC's own criteria.

This paper does.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CLIS-WPI/ngso-sharing-incidence}} We model the
constellations both \emph{as filed} (the orbits
in the operators' FCC filings) and \emph{as deployed} (the satellites actually in
orbit today), apply
the rule's exact criteria at every latitude, and follow the interference from
the trigger to the protection limits. The answer is a twist: the rule's
protection is real only when the victim can \emph{hand off} to another
satellite, and the protected system's own filed design can take that option
away. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A general model of NGSO co-frequency interference and of the
\S\,25.261 criteria, instantiated for Starlink, OneWeb, and Amazon Leo
(formerly Project Kuiper), which reduces to a single co-frequency user-downlink
pair, Starlink--OneWeb.
\item To our knowledge, the first latitude-resolved measurement of how often the \S\,25.261 event
fires and whether the \S\,25.261(d) limits hold, as filed and as deployed, with
and without satellite diversity, at two sourced Starlink power levels.
\item A closed-form approximation that turns the regulatory power criterion into
a geometric one through an equivalent off-axis angle, independent of terminal
size, validated against the simulation.
\item Evidence that compliance hinges on satellite diversity and on the victim's
elevation mask, neither of which the rule mentions, and that the burden falls
on the very system the rule protects.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:model} develops the model, Section~\ref{sec:setup} instantiates
it, Section~\ref{sec:analytic} derives the approximation,
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws the regulatory implications.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\emph{Systems and geometry.} We consider a set $\mathcal{K}$ of NGSO systems.
System $k \in \mathcal{K}$ consists of shells $\mathcal{S}_k$ (altitude, inclination,
planes, satellites per plane) and is authorized in a processing round
$\rho_k$. Satellite positions $\mathbf{p}_j(t)$ evolve over discrete time
$t \in \mathcal{T}$. Earth stations $g \in \mathcal{G}$ are placed at known
geodetic positions. Satellite $j$ is visible from $g$ when its elevation
$\epsilon_{g,j}(t)$ is at least the minimum elevation $\epsilon_k^{\min}$ of its
system; $\mathcal{V}_{k}(g,t)$ denotes the visible satellites of system $k$. A
station of system $k$ is served by $s_k(g,t) \in \mathcal{V}_k(g,t)$, selected by
a policy of the operator (e.g., highest elevation).

\emph{Co-frequency pairs.} Each system holds authorized frequency ranges
$\mathcal{F}_k^{\ell}$ for each link type $\ell$ (user/gateway, up/down). An
ordered pair $(A,B)$ is screened on link type $\ell$ only if
$\mathcal{F}_A^{\ell} \cap \mathcal{F}_B^{\ell} \neq \emptyset$; the coupling is
evaluated at the centre frequency $f$ of the overlap, with wavelength $\lambda$.

\emph{Interference and the in-line event.} For a victim station $g$ of system $A$
served by $s_A$, the aggregate interference from system $B$ is
\begin{equation}
I_g(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}_B(g,t)} \frac{\mathrm{EIRP}_j\,
g_j(\phi_{g,j})\, G_{\text{rx}}(\theta_{g,j})}{L(r_{g,j})},
\label{eq:agg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{EIRP}_j$ is the peak equivalent isotropically radiated power
(EIRP) density of satellite $j$, $g_j(\phi)$ its
normalized transmit pattern at the off-axis angle $\phi_{g,j}$ toward $g$,
$G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ the victim receive gain at the angle $\theta_{g,j}$
between $s_A$ and $j$ seen from $g$, and $L(r)=(4\pi r/\lambda)^2$ the free-space
loss at slant range $r_{g,j}$. With noise density $N = kT_{\text{sys}}$ (per unit
bandwidth), the in-line event is
\begin{equation}
E_g(t) = \mathbb{1}\{\Delta T/T = I_g(t)/N > \delta\},
\label{eq:event}
\end{equation}
with $\delta$ the regulatory threshold. Three interferer sets bracket the
unknown beam plans of system $B$: \emph{realistic}, where $B$'s serving satellite
for the site radiates its main beam ($g_j = 1$) and every other visible $B$
satellite contributes through its sidelobe; \emph{co-located}, where only $B$'s
serving satellite is counted; and an \emph{upper bound}, where every visible
$B$ satellite radiates at peak toward $g$.

\emph{Interferer power models.} Two models are considered. Under
\emph{constant EIRP}, $\mathrm{EIRP}_j$ is fixed. Under \emph{constant PFD}, the
operator holds the surface power-flux density (PFD) at $\mathrm{PFD}_0$ while steering,
so that
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EIRP}_j(r) = \mathrm{PFD}_0 + 10\log_{10}(4\pi r^2) \quad
[\text{dBW/MHz}],
\label{eq:pfd}
\end{equation}
capped at its value at the range $r_{\max}$ of the minimum service elevation.

\emph{Protection metrics.} Let $c_g(t)$ be the victim's carrier-to-noise ratio and
$\gamma_g(t) = c_g(t)/(1+I_g(t)/N)$ its signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR). With an achievable-rate map $R(\cdot)$
(e.g., the Shannon rate with an implementation margin) and a C/N threshold
$\gamma_0$, the time-weighted throughput degradation and the absolute change in
unavailability are
\begin{equation}
D_g = 1 - \frac{\overline{R(\gamma_g)}}{\overline{R(c_g)}},\qquad
\Delta U_g = \Pr\{\gamma_g < \gamma_0\} - \Pr\{c_g < \gamma_0\},
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\cdot}$ and $\Pr\{\cdot\}$ are taken over $\mathcal{T}$ and an
unserved instant counts as unavailable. A link qualifies for protection only if
its baseline availability $\Pr\{c_g \ge \gamma_0\}$ is at least $a_{\min}$. The
rule is met when $D_g \le D_{\max}$ and $\Delta U_g \le U_{\max}$ at every
qualifying station. Both metrics are invariant to the channel bandwidth, since
$I/N$ and $c$ are per-unit-bandwidth ratios and $D_g$ is a rate ratio.

\emph{Satellite diversity.} With diversity (a \emph{handoff} to another
satellite), at each $t$ the victim may replace
$s_A$ by any $j' \in \mathcal{V}_A(g,t)$ for which (\ref{eq:event}) is not
triggered; the event is \emph{unresolvable} if no such $j'$ exists. $D_g$ and
$\Delta U_g$ are then evaluated on the link actually used.

\section{Regulatory Instantiation and Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\emph{Rule parameters.} Under 47 CFR~25.261, $\delta = \DttThreshold$
($I/N > \InThresholdDb$~dB)~\cite{cfr25261}, and for \S\,25.261(d)
$D_{\max} = \DegradeLimitPct\%$, $U_{\max} = \UnavailLimitPct\%$,
$\gamma_0 = \CnGateDb$~dB, and $a_{\min} = \RuleBaselineAvailPct\%$~\cite{fcc24117}.
\S\,25.261(d) is evaluated only where it legally applies, i.e., for a victim
from an earlier processing round than the interferer.

\emph{Systems.} We instantiate the model for Starlink, OneWeb, and Amazon Leo
(formerly Project Kuiper; ``Kuiper'' below, as in its filings).
Screening their authorized bands by link type leaves a single co-frequency
user-downlink pair, Starlink and OneWeb in \SharedBandLoGhz--\SharedBandHiGhz~GHz,
coupled at $f = \SharedFreqGhz$~GHz; Kuiper's user downlink is in Ka band.
OneWeb (2016 round) is the protected victim of Starlink Gen2 (2020 round).
\emph{As filed}, the systems are Walker-Delta shells (evenly spaced orbital planes, the
standard model of a filed constellation) propagated with a two-body
model plus $J_2$ secular drift over \EventNPhasings\ independent constellation
phasings (random rotations of each constellation in orbit, which serve as
statistical replications). \emph{As deployed}, positions come from an October 2026 snapshot of the public CelesTrak
orbital-element catalog~\cite{celestrak} (\NStarlinkRaw\ Starlink and \NOnewebRaw\ OneWeb
objects, filtered to \NStarlinkOp\ and \NOnewebOp\ at operational altitude) over
\NStartTimes\ independent start times. The \NStations\ stations lie at latitudes
\LatMinDeg--\LatMaxDeg$^\circ$ in \LatStepDeg$^\circ$ steps on \NLongitudes\
meridians, and each is served by the highest-elevation visible satellite. The
victim gain follows the International Telecommunication Union (ITU-R)
earth-station pattern S.1428~\cite{itu1428} and the interferer follows the ITU-R satellite pattern
S.1528~\cite{itu1528}; $R(\cdot)$ is the Shannon rate with a \ImplMarginDb~dB
margin. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists all parameters and their sources.

\emph{Interferer power.} Starlink Ku holds a constant surface PFD while
steering~\cite{fcc2291}, so (\ref{eq:pfd}) applies with two sourced anchors for
$\mathrm{PFD}_0$, both taken at the \PfdAnchorElevDeg$^\circ$ service edge
(the lowest elevation at which Starlink serves users). (a)
\emph{Gen2 filed maximum} (primary): the 47 CFR~25.208(b) limit,
$\PfdLimitDbw$~dBW/m$^2$/MHz~\cite{cfr25208} (model $\PrimaryPfdDbw$); as a cross-check, the filed Gen2 EIRP
density varies with altitude~\cite{fcc2291}, and its top value,
\StarlinkFiledTopDbw~dBW/MHz after conversion from the per-4-kHz bandwidth in
which FCC filings state Ku-band densities, gives $\FiledPfdDbw$, within
\FiledPfdOffsetDb~dB of the limit; the bottom of the same range is used as a
sensitivity case (``Gen2 range low end''). (b) \emph{Gen1 design point}: the constant PFD of the 2016
filing~\cite[Table~A.7-1]{spacex2016}, $\GenOnePfdDbw$~dBW/m$^2$/MHz,
\PfdOffsetGenOneDb~dB below the limit; for Gen1 itself \S\,25.261(c), not (d),
applies, so (b) serves as a sourced lower operating point of the interferer.
Section~25.208(b) covers only \PfdScopeLoGhz--\PfdScopeHiGhz~GHz of the shared
band. OneWeb is constant-EIRP with fixed beams. Replacing the channel bandwidth
by \BwCheckMhz~MHz~\cite[Table~3]{delportillo2019} leaves every metric unchanged
(largest difference \BwMaxAbsDiffPct\ percentage points, pp), as expected from the bandwidth
invariance above.

\emph{Aggregation.} Every \S\,25.261(d) number is the worst qualifying station
per replication (phasing or start time), averaged across replications, with a
\CiPct\% $t$-interval; a limit is \emph{exceeded} only if the whole interval lies
above it and \emph{met} only if it lies below.

\section{Analytic Approximation}
\label{sec:analytic}
Why should a power criterion depend on geometry at all? Under constant PFD it
reduces to one.
For a constant-PFD interferer (\ref{eq:pfd}), the main-beam contribution
$\mathrm{PFD}_0\,\lambda^2/(4\pi)\,G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ is range-independent, so
in the co-located case the event (\ref{eq:event}) depends only on the victim
off-axis angle $\theta$ and reduces to $\theta < \theta^{*}$, where (in dB)
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{rx}}(\theta^{*}) = \delta_{\text{dB}} + N - \mathrm{PFD}_0 -
10\log_{10}(\lambda^2/4\pi).
\label{eq:thetastar}
\end{equation}
Whenever $\theta^{*}$ falls in the sidelobe envelope of the receive pattern,
which for S.1428 is $G = 32 - 25\log_{10}\theta$ ($1^\circ$--$48^\circ$), the
aperture cancels. For the instantiation of Section~\ref{sec:setup} this gives
$\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$ for the OneWeb victim (spread \AnalyticThetaSpreadDeg$^\circ$ across
\EirpHeadDiam--\AperMaxM~m). This explains why the event fraction barely depends
on the terminal size: as deployed, its peak ranges only from
\BindMinApertureDepPct\% to \BindMaxApertureDepPct\% across the apertures
studied. The co-located event probability is then
\begin{equation}
P = v_A v_B\,\Pr\{\angle(\hat u_A,\hat u_B) < \theta^{*}\},
\label{eq:pevent}
\end{equation}
where $v_k = 1 - e^{-n_k}$ is the probability that system $k$ has a satellite in
view ($n_k$ its mean visible count) and each serving direction $\hat u$ is the nearest
of $n$ satellites uniform in the ground footprint, mapped to the look angle. The
two serving directions are independent: the simulated marginals reproduce the
simulated separation probability within \DiagnosisIndepMaxErrPct~pp, whereas a marginal uniform in the look-angle cap errs by up to
\DiagnosisOldMaxErrPct~pp. Fig.~\ref{fig:analytic} compares (\ref{eq:pevent})
with the simulated co-located as-filed curve: the model is within
\AnalyticMaxAbsErrPct~pp (signed error $\AnalyticSignedMinPct$\ to
\AnalyticSignedMaxPct~pp); the error varies with latitude, with under-prediction
at \AnalyticUnderLatLo--\AnalyticUnderLatHi$^\circ$ and over-prediction at
\AnalyticOverLowLatLo--\AnalyticOverLowLatHi$^\circ$ and
\AnalyticOverHighLatLo--\AnalyticOverHighLatHi$^\circ$. The model captures the
magnitude and latitude trend of the event fraction; the simulation remains the
primary result.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
We now follow one OneWeb user through the rule, from the trigger to the
protection limits, and ask at each step what the rule promises and what the
geometry delivers.

\emph{The trigger fires often, and mostly on OneWeb.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:events} shows the fraction of time the $\Delta T/T$ criterion is
exceeded. For a OneWeb user it peaks at
\BindDepWorstPct$\pm$\BindDepWorstCi\% at \BindDepWorstLat$^\circ$ latitude as
deployed (\BindFilWorstPct\% as filed). A Starlink user is hit far less: its peak
is \BindDepStarWorstPct\% as deployed and \BindFilStarWorstPct\% as filed, so the
OneWeb user carries \BurdenRatioDep$\times$ (as deployed) and
\BurdenRatioFil$\times$ (as filed) the exposure. The asymmetry has a physical
cause. As victim, OneWeb at \OnewebAltKm~km looks through the PFD-limited beams
of the lower, denser Starlink shell at \StarlinkAltKm~km; as interferer, its
longer slant range and lower EIRP density weaken its own contribution. The
co-located variant is close to the realistic one
(\BindDepColoPct$\pm$\BindDepColoCi\% and \BindFilColoPct\%), so a single beam,
Starlink's serving beam over the site, does most of the damage; the upper bound
reaches \UpperBoundEventPct\% by construction.

\emph{A narrow geometric screen would miss most of it.}
A common shortcut declares an in-line event only when the two serving satellites
are nearly aligned. With a \ThetaScreenDeg$^\circ$ separation threshold, that
screen fires only \ThetaColoPeakPct\% of the time at its peak
(\ThetaColoPeakLat$^\circ$) and \ThetaColoEquatorPct\% at the equator, as filed.
Equation~(\ref{eq:thetastar}) explains the gap: at the PFD limit, Starlink's
serving beam pushes OneWeb's $\Delta T/T$ over the threshold through the
terminal's sidelobes out to $\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$, far
beyond a few degrees. Geometric definitions therefore understate how often the
rule actually applies.

\emph{Without handoff, the protection fails.}
Suppose the OneWeb user simply stays on its serving satellite. At Starlink's Gen2
filed maximum, all OneWeb links qualify for protection (\QualOneCases\ of
\QualOneTotal\ cases as filed), and both limits are broken in both geometries
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}): throughput degrades by
\DegrDepMaxPct$\pm$\DegrDepMaxCi\% as deployed and
\DegrFilMaxPct$\pm$\DegrFilMaxCi\% as filed, against a \DegradeLimitPct\% limit,
and availability drops by \UnavailIncDepPct$\pm$\UnavailIncDepCi\% and
\UnavailIncPct$\pm$\UnavailIncCi\%, against \UnavailLimitPct\%. For comparison
only (not a compliance test, since OneWeb is the earlier-round system), a
Starlink user loses \DegrDepStarPct$\pm$\DegrDepStarCi\% of throughput.

\emph{How much power is too much.}
Is the violation an artifact of assuming maximum power?
Fig.~\ref{fig:pfd} and Table~\ref{tab:cases} lower it. The limits are still
broken \PfdOffsetThreeDb~dB below the Gen2 maximum. Only at Starlink's Gen1 design
power, \PfdOffsetGenOneDb~dB below, does the throughput loss fall within the
limit (\TabGenOneDegrNoDivPct$\pm$\TabGenOneDegrNoDivCi\%), and even there the
availability change (\TabGenOneUnavNoDivPct$\pm$\TabGenOneUnavNoDivCi\%) cannot
be distinguished from the \UnavailLimitPct\% threshold. At the low end of the
filed Gen2 range, throughput passes narrowly
(\TabLowDegrNoDivPct$\pm$\TabLowDegrNoDivCi\%) but availability does not
(\TabLowUnavNoDivPct$\pm$\TabLowUnavNoDivCi\%).  Three further cases also break both limits: Starlink's
user-beam EIRP as reported for its Gen1 architecture in~\cite{delportillo2019};
constant-EIRP instead of constant-PFD steering; and generic noise temperatures of
\NoiseAssumedOneK/\NoiseAssumedStarK~K in place of the values
of~\cite{delportillo2019}. Without handoff, compliance therefore requires Starlink to operate at
least \PfdOffsetGenOneDb~dB below its Gen2 filed maximum.

\emph{Handoff rescues compliance.}
Now let the OneWeb user switch to another visible OneWeb satellite whenever its
own link is hit. The picture changes completely: throughput degradation falls to
\DegrDepDivPct$\pm$\DegrDepDivCi\% as deployed and
\DegrFilDivPct$\pm$\DegrFilDivCi\% as filed, and the availability change to
\UnavailDepDivPct\% and \UnavailFilDivPct\%. Both limits are met, and they stay
met in every row of Table~\ref{tab:cases} with a \StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ mask,
including constant-EIRP steering (largest degradation
\TabConstEirpDegrDivPct$\pm$\TabConstEirpDegrDivCi\%). The rule's promise holds,
but it is satellite diversity, not the rule, that keeps it.

\emph{\ldots unless the victim cannot look low.}
Handoff needs a second satellite in view, and OneWeb's own filed design
restricts where it looks: the system keeps a satellite above
\OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ for its users~\cite[Sec.~2.2]{delportillo2019}.
Applying this mask to the as-deployed OneWeb constellation tells two stories.
First, coverage itself thins out: the time with no OneWeb satellite above the
mask is \MinElevNoneEquatorPct\% at the equator, and only the stations at
\MinElevQualLatLo--\MinElevQualLatHi$^\circ$ (\MinElevQualStations\ of
\NStations) reach \RuleBaselineAvailPct\% baseline availability; below
\MinElevQualLatLo$^\circ$, \S\,25.261(d) protection is not even defined. Second,
where protection is defined, the escape route is narrow. At the worst qualifying
station (\MinElevWorstLat$^\circ$) the serving satellite is the same as with a
\StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ mask (none is above the mask only
\MinElevWorstNonePct\% of the time), so the no-diversity metrics do not change;
but the mean number of OneWeb satellites above \OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ at
the qualifying latitudes is only \MinElevNAboveMin--\MinElevNAboveMax, with a
10th percentile of \MinElevNAboveTenMin: often there is nowhere to go. With
diversity, throughput degradation
(\TabMinElevFiftyFiveDegrDivPct$\pm$\TabMinElevFiftyFiveDegrDivCi\%) can no longer
be distinguished from the limit, and the availability change
(\TabMinElevFiftyFiveUnavDivPct$\pm$\TabMinElevFiftyFiveUnavDivCi\%) exceeds it.
This matches the early observation that OneWeb's fixed, non-steerable beams leave
it only a passive role in in-line interference
coordination~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{delportillo2019}. We read
\OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ as the filed design elevation and
\StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ as an operational assumption; together they bracket
current practice.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Three implications follow for the design and enforcement of \S\,25.261.
\emph{Protection is borrowed from the victim.} At the same interferer power, the
limits are broken or met depending on whether the victim can hand off. Handoff is
the incumbent's operational choice, yet compliance is a test of the entrant; a
compliance showing that assumes diversity quietly transfers part of the burden to
the system the rule protects. \emph{The availability gate interacts with design.}
Protection applies only to links with \RuleBaselineAvailPct\% baseline
availability. Under its filed elevation mask, the protected system qualifies for protection
only at higher latitudes, so the gate can withdraw protection exactly where
coverage is thin. \emph{Measure the criterion, not a proxy.} Geometric in-line
screens understate how often the $\Delta T/T$ criterion applies, because the
trigger reaches deep into the victim terminal's sidelobes; regulatory showings
should evaluate the criterion itself.


\section{Limitations}
(a) The S.1528 sidelobe of non-serving interferers assumes nadir-pointing beams.
(b) Consumer terminals are flat panels; the S.1428 dish pattern approximates them.
(c) Each station uses its highest-elevation satellite; operators' selection
policies differ. (d) Diversity is unlimited and assumes knowledge of the
interfering serving satellite. (e) Only the user downlink is modelled.
(f) The OneWeb EIRP is taken from~\cite{delportillo2019}; its constant-EIRP
steering and the \StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ minimum elevation are assumptions;
the \OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ result uses the as-deployed geometry. (g) The
primary Starlink power is the regulatory PFD maximum; Table~\ref{tab:cases}
bounds lower power. (h) The as-deployed geometry is a single snapshot, so its
confidence intervals reflect temporal variation within that snapshot, not
variation across snapshots.

\section{Conclusion}
The FCC's NGSO sharing rule promises an earlier-round system that a later-round
system will cost it at most \DegradeLimitPct\% of throughput and
\UnavailLimitPct\% of availability. For the Starlink--OneWeb Ku user downlink, we
find that the rule's trigger fires often, up to
\BindDepWorstPct$\pm$\BindDepWorstCi\% of the time for a OneWeb user and
\BurdenRatioDep$\times$ more than for a Starlink user. Without handoff, Starlink
Gen2 at its filed maximum breaks both limits, and only its lower Gen1 design
power brings throughput back within the limit. With handoff, both limits are
met, unless OneWeb's own filed \OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ elevation mask
removes the satellites it would hand off to. The protection the rule promises is
therefore delivered by satellite diversity and constrained by the victim's
design, neither of which the rule mentions. Future work will extend the analysis
to gateway links, capacity-limited diversity, and new co-frequency entrants.
