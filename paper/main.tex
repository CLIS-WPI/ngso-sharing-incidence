\begin{abstract}
Avoiding interference by switching satellites can move a user to a weaker
link. For co-frequency low-Earth-orbit systems, this raises an open question:
how much does the protected system's satellite selection
policy affect an assessment against the FCC's interference protection
limits? We investigate it for Starlink Gen2 and OneWeb under 47 CFR~25.261, in
filed and deployed constellation geometries. Our model recomputes the desired signal
and aggregate interference for each candidate satellite and compares three
deliberately simple victim policies against a common interference-free
baseline: no interference-driven handoff, event-avoiding handoff, and rate-aware
handoff, which during an event keeps the interfered link if it offers a higher
rate than every event-free alternate. In the deployed geometry, under clear skies, Starlink's filed
maximum power-flux density (PFD), a 25$^\circ$ elevation mask, and unrestricted, lossless
switching, these policies yield mean worst-station throughput degradations
of \DepGenTwoNoHoDegrPct\%, \DepGenTwoEvAvDegrPct\%, and
\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrPct\%, respectively, against a
\DegradeLimitPct\% limit, at the cost of \DepDwellRateMaxNTenSwitchesPerHour\
switches per hour on average for rate-aware handoff versus \IntRateMaxBaseSwitchesPerHour\ for
the baseline. Event-avoiding handoff narrowly exceeds the limit
(it meets it as filed) because avoiding every in-line event can select a weaker desired link;
rate-aware handoff meets it by retaining the interfered link when
that link offers the higher rate. Both handoff policies meet the
availability limit under these assumptions, but fail it with a 60~s
minimum dwell time or a 55$^\circ$ elevation mask. A separate upper-bound
interruption analysis indicates subsecond availability tolerances per extra
switch. Compatibility
assessments should therefore state and test the incumbent's assumed selection
policy and its switching constraints.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Ku-band sky over a user terminal is now crowded: our snapshot counts
\NStarlinkOperational\ operational Starlink satellites, \NStarlinkGenTwo\ of them
second-generation, and \NOnewebOp\ OneWeb satellites, all transmitting to users
in the same band. When one system's satellite crosses the line of sight of the
other system's user, someone has to give way. The obvious defense for that user
is to switch to another of its own satellites, but the alternate is often lower
and farther away, so escaping the interference can cost more than tolerating it.

This tradeoff matters for regulation. In the United States, sharing among
non-geostationary-orbit (NGSO) systems is governed by the Federal Communications
Commission (FCC) rule in Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations,
47 CFR~25.261~\cite{cfr25261}, which protects a system
licensed in an earlier \emph{processing round} (the \emph{incumbent}) from one
licensed later (the \emph{entrant}). Before operating, the entrant must coordinate
or submit a \emph{compatibility showing} that it costs the incumbent at most
\DegradeLimitPct\% of throughput and \UnavailLimitPct\% of availability
(the \emph{protection limits}, \S\,25.261(d))~\cite{fcc24117}, and the FCC lets that analysis \emph{assume} the
incumbent's satellite selection strategy when the actual one is not
provided~\cite{fcc24117}. How much this assumption shapes the verdict has not been
measured. We answer this question for the only co-frequency user-downlink pair
among Starlink, OneWeb, and Amazon Leo (formerly Project Kuiper): OneWeb, from
the 2016 round~\cite{fcc1777}, and Starlink Gen2, from the 2020
round~\cite{fcc2291}.

\emph{The rule in brief.} Two systems are \emph{co-frequency} when they may use
the same frequencies for the same type of link. For such systems, \S\,25.261 has
two regimes. (i)~Between systems of the \emph{same} round, it acts only during an
\emph{in-line event}, that is, while interference raises a receiver's noise
temperature by more than \DttThresholdPct\% ($\Delta T/T$); the systems then split
the shared band, each using its own portion, until the event ends
(\S\,25.261(c))~\cite{cfr25261,fcc2329}. OneWeb and Starlink Gen1, both from the
2016 round~\cite{fcc1838}, fall under this regime. (ii)~Between rounds, the
entrant must meet the two limits above on every incumbent link that, without
interference, has a carrier-to-noise ratio (C/N) of at least \CnGateDb~dB at least
\RuleBaselineAvailPct\% of the time. This protection expires ten years after
the later round's first grant, after which (i) applies. A 2026 order on NGSO--geostationary sharing~\cite{fcc2626} left
these criteria unchanged.

\emph{Related work.} Interference between the OneWeb and SpaceX user downlinks
was anticipated in~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{delportillo2019}, its practical impact among
dense NGSO constellations was examined in~\cite{braun2019},
and~\cite{kim2025,kim2026selection} designed satellite selection by the entrant to
enable coexistence in Ka band. None evaluates the FCC's own criteria or the
effect of the \emph{victim's} handoff on the verdict, which we study for the
Ku-band user downlink. We address this gap with three
contributions:\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CLIS-WPI/ngso-sharing-incidence}}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Model:} a general model of NGSO co-frequency interference, the
FCC criteria, and victim handoff policies, evaluated \emph{as filed}
(orbits in the operators' filings) and \emph{as deployed} (satellites in orbit
today).
\item \emph{Policy dependence:} as deployed, leaving every interfered satellite
narrowly exceeds the throughput limit, whereas staying while the interfered link
is the better one meets it; we map where each policy fails under switching
constraints.
\item \emph{Event incidence:} to our knowledge, the first latitude-resolved
estimate of how often the modeled in-line event fires, with an analytic
approximation that reduces the power criterion to an off-axis angle,
aperture-independent under the ITU-R envelope, plus a link-budget criterion for when switching pays.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\emph{Systems and geometry.} We consider a set $\mathcal{K}$ of NGSO systems.
System $k \in \mathcal{K}$ consists of shells $\mathcal{S}_k$ (altitude,
inclination, planes, satellites per plane) and is authorized in a processing
round $\rho_k$. Satellite positions evolve over discrete time
$t \in \mathcal{T}$, and earth stations $g \in \mathcal{G}$ are placed at known
geodetic positions. Satellite $j$ is visible from $g$ when its elevation is at
least the minimum elevation $\epsilon_k^{\min}$ of its system;
$\mathcal{V}_{k}(g,t)$ denotes the visible satellites of system $k$.

\emph{Co-frequency pairs.} A pair $(A,B)$ is screened on link type $\ell$
(user/gateway, up/down) only if their authorized ranges overlap,
$\mathcal{F}_A^{\ell} \cap \mathcal{F}_B^{\ell} \neq \emptyset$, and coupled at the
overlap's center frequency $f$ (wavelength $\lambda$).

\emph{Signal, interference, and the in-line event.} A victim station $g$ of
system $A$ served by satellite $s \in \mathcal{V}_A(g,t)$ has carrier-to-noise
ratio and aggregate interference from system $B$
\begin{gather}
c_g(t;s) = \frac{\mathrm{EIRP}_s\, G_{\text{rx}}(0)}{L(r_{g,s})\,N}, \label{eq:cn}\\
I_g(t;s) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}_B(g,t)} \frac{\mathrm{EIRP}_j\,
F_j(\phi_{g,j})\, G_{\text{rx}}(\theta_{g,j}^{s})}{L(r_{g,j})},
\label{eq:agg}
\end{gather}
where $\mathrm{EIRP}$ is the peak equivalent isotropically radiated power density
(linear), $F_j(\phi)$ the normalized transmit pattern of satellite $j$ at the
off-axis angle $\phi_{g,j}$ toward $g$, $G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ the victim
receive gain at the angle $\theta_{g,j}^{s}$ between $s$ and $j$ seen from $g$
(the terminal points at $s$), $L(r)=(4\pi r/\lambda)^2$ the free-space loss at
slant range $r$, and $N = kT_{\text{sys}}$ the noise power spectral density;
$\mathrm{EIRP}$, $I_g$, and $N$ are all per unit bandwidth. In (\ref{eq:cn}), the
serving satellite $s$ is assumed to point a beam at $g$, so the victim receives
its peak EIRP and the terminal applies its peak gain $G_{\text{rx}}(0)$. The
in-line event on link $s$ is
\begin{equation}
E_g(t;s)=
\begin{cases}
1, & I_g(t;s)/N > \delta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:event}
\end{equation}
with $I_g(t;s)/N = \Delta T/T$ and $\delta$ the regulatory threshold. Three interferer sets bracket the unknown
beam plans of system $B$: \emph{realistic}, where $B$'s serving satellite for the
site (its highest-elevation visible satellite) radiates its main beam ($F_j = 1$)
and every other visible $B$ satellite contributes through its sidelobe, with
$\phi_{g,j}$ measured from nadir;
\emph{co-located}, where only $B$'s serving satellite is counted; and an
\emph{upper bound}, where every visible $B$ satellite radiates at peak toward $g$.

\emph{Interferer power models.} Under \emph{constant EIRP}, $\mathrm{EIRP}_j$ is
fixed. Under \emph{constant PFD}, the operator holds the surface power-flux
density (PFD) at $\mathrm{PFD}_0$ while steering, so that (in dB)
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EIRP}_{j,\text{dB}}(r) = \mathrm{PFD}_{0,\text{dB}} + 10\log_{10}(4\pi r^2),
\label{eq:pfd}
\end{equation}
capped at its value at the range $r_{\max}$ of the minimum service elevation.

\emph{Handoff policies.} The baseline policy serves $g$ from the highest-elevation
visible satellite $s_0(g,t)$. A handoff policy considers switching only while the
baseline link is in event, $E_g(t;s_0)=1$, and only to \emph{admissible}
alternates $j \in \mathcal{V}_A(g,t)\setminus\{s_0\}$ with $E_g(t;j)=0$, each
evaluated with its own link budget (\ref{eq:cn})--(\ref{eq:agg}). With
$\gamma_g(t;s) = c_g(t;s)/(1+I_g(t;s)/N)$ the SINR,
\emph{event-avoiding} handoff always leaves $s_0$ for the admissible alternate
with the highest $\gamma$, whereas \emph{rate-aware} handoff picks the higher $\gamma$ among
the admissible alternates and the degraded link $s_0$, i.e., it maximizes the rate
over this restricted set only (both policies share the admissible set; admitting
alternates in event could, with unrestricted switching, only lower its degradation). If no alternate
is admissible, the event is \emph{unresolvable} and the victim stays on $s_0$.
The decision is made anew at every time step: if $s_0$ is not in event, the
victim uses $s_0$, returning to it as soon as an event ends; otherwise it applies
its policy. Two constraints restrict switching: only the $K$ highest-elevation
satellites are candidates, and under a minimum dwell time $N_{\text{s}}$ the victim
keeps its current satellite for $N_{\text{s}}$ seconds after any switch (including
a return to $s_0$ or a change of $s_0$), unless that satellite leaves the candidate set. A handoff interruption of $\tau$ seconds is added after
simulation: with $E$ the excess of the policy's switches per hour over the
baseline's, the interrupted time fraction is $f = E\tau/3600$, and
$\Delta U_{g,\tau} = \Delta U_g + f$ and $D_{g,\tau} = 1-(1-D_g)(1-f)$, per station
and day, using the policy's time-averaged rate as the rate lost during an
interruption; overlap with existing outages is
not removed, so $\Delta U_{g,\tau}$ is an upper bound.

\emph{Protection metrics.} With $s(t)$ the satellite used, the rate map
$R(\gamma) = \log_2(1+\gamma/m)$ ($R=0$ if unserved; $m$ an implementation
margin), and a C/N threshold $\gamma_0$, the \emph{relative} loss of mean
throughput $D_g$ and the \emph{absolute} rise in unavailable time $\Delta U_g$ are
\begin{gather}
D_g = 1 - \frac{\overline{R(\gamma_g(t;s(t)))}}{\overline{R(c_g(t;s_0))}},\label{eq:degr}\\
\Delta U_g = \Pr\{\gamma_g(t;s(t)) < \gamma_0\} - \Pr\{c_g(t;s_0) < \gamma_0\},
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{gather}
over $t\in\mathcal{T}$, with an unserved instant counted as unavailable. The baseline is the interference-free link
under the baseline policy. A link
qualifies for protection only if its baseline availability
$\Pr\{c_g \ge \gamma_0\}$ is at least $a_{\min}$, and the rule is met when
$D_g \le D_{\max}$ and $\Delta U_g \le U_{\max}$.

\section{Evaluation Setup}

\emph{Regulatory parameters and statistics.} The rule maps onto our outputs as

\section{Analytic Approximation}
\label{sec:analytic}
Why should a power criterion depend on geometry at all? Under constant PFD
(\ref{eq:pfd}), the main-beam interference
$\mathrm{PFD}_0\,\lambda^2/(4\pi)\,G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ is range-independent,
so in the co-located case the event (\ref{eq:event}) depends only on the victim
off-axis angle $\theta$ and reduces to $\theta < \theta^{*}$, where, with $N$
and $\mathrm{PFD}_0$ in matching per-MHz units,
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{rx}}(\theta^{*}) = \delta_{\text{dB}} + N_{\text{dB}} -
\mathrm{PFD}_{0,\text{dB}} - 10\log_{10}(\lambda^2/4\pi).
\label{eq:thetastar}
\end{equation}
Whenever $\theta^{*}$ falls in the sidelobe envelope of the receive pattern, which
for S.1428 is $G = 32 - 25\log_{10}\theta$ ($1^\circ$--$48^\circ$), the aperture
cancels (a property of this envelope, not of real antennas): for OneWeb, $\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$ (spread
\AnalyticThetaSpreadDeg$^\circ$ across \EirpHeadDiam--\AperMaxM~m), which explains
why the event fraction barely depends on terminal size. The co-located event
probability is then
\begin{equation}
P = v_A v_B\,\Pr\{\angle(\hat u_A,\hat u_B) < \theta^{*}\},
\label{eq:pevent}
\end{equation}
where $n_k$ is the mean visible count of system $k$ from its Walker latitude
density, and we approximate visibility as Poisson ($v_k = 1 - e^{-n_k}$), each
serving direction $\hat u_k$ as the nearest of $n_k$ satellites uniform in the
footprint, and the two directions as independent, ignoring the structured
orbits; (\ref{eq:pevent}) covers only the co-located event, not the aggregate
(\ref{eq:agg}); the serving beam dominates, though (peak \BindDepColoPct\%
co-located vs.\ \BindDepWorstPct\% realistic). On the filed Gen2 shells,
(\ref{eq:pevent}) is within $\AnalyticSignedMinInclPct$ to \AnalyticSignedMaxInclPct\
percentage points (pp) of the simulation up to \AnalyticInclLatDeg$^\circ$
latitude and errs by up to \AnalyticMaxAbsBeyondPct~pp beyond the Gen2
inclinations; it gives only the order of magnitude, whereas (\ref{eq:thetastar}) is exact under constant PFD.

\emph{When does switching pay?} For OneWeb's constant EIRP, $c_g(t;s)$ in
(\ref{eq:cn}) depends on $s$ only through $L(r_{g,s})$, so leaving the interfered
$s_0$ for an event-free alternate $j$ raises the SINR only if
\begin{equation}
10\log_{10}\frac{1+I_g(t;s_0)/N}{1+I_g(t;j)/N} > 20\log_{10}\frac{r_{g,j}}{r_{g,s_0}},
\label{eq:switch}
\end{equation}
i.e., the interference relief must exceed the extra path loss. At the median
event $I/N$ of $\DepInServMedDb$~dB, the left side is a fraction of a decibel, so a
more distant alternate rarely pays; rate-aware handoff applies
(\ref{eq:switch}), event-avoiding handoff ignores it. The resulting throughput
and availability are evaluated by simulation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
We follow one OneWeb user through the rule, from the trigger to the protection
limits, with Starlink Gen2 at its filed maximum unless stated otherwise.

\emph{The modeled trigger fires often, and mostly on OneWeb.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:events} shows the fraction of time with $\Delta T/T$ above the
threshold. For a OneWeb user it peaks at \BindDepWorstPct$\pm$\BindDepWorstCi\% at
\BindDepWorstLat$^\circ$ as deployed (\BindFilWorstPct\% as filed), whereas a
Starlink user peaks at \BindDepStarWorstPct\%: OneWeb carries
\BurdenRatioDep$\times$ the modeled exposure. These are modeled
probabilities, not measured exposure: actual beam activity is unknown. OneWeb, at \OnewebAltKm~km, looks through the
lower, denser Starlink shells, while its own longer range and lower EIRP density
weaken it as interferer. The event fraction follows Gen2 visibility, highest at mid-latitudes
(\VisDepLatForty\ satellites at 40$^\circ$) and collapsing beyond the 53$^\circ$
inclination; at the equator it exceeds the as-filed value with fewer visible
satellites (\VisDepLatZero\ vs.\ \VisFilLatZero), so the interferer count alone does not set it.

\emph{A narrow geometric screen would miss most of it.}
A common shortcut flags an in-line event when the two serving satellites are
within a fixed angle. At 1, 2, 5, and 10$^\circ$ that screen fires only
\ThetaScreenOnePeakPct, \ThetaScreenTwoPeakPct, \ThetaScreenFivePeakPct, and
\ThetaScreenTenPeakPct\% of the time at its peak (co-located, as deployed),
whereas a screen at $\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$ from
(\ref{eq:thetastar}) reproduces the $\Delta T/T$ peak (\ThetaScreenStarPeakPct\
vs.\ \BindDepColoPct\%).

\emph{Without handoff, the modeled showing fails.}

\emph{Which handoff decides throughput.}
Now let the OneWeb user switch satellites during events (Table~\ref{tab:handoff},
Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}). All three policies share one run, one interference-free
baseline, and a full link budget for every candidate, so differences come from
the policy alone. With a 25$^\circ$ mask and unrestricted, lossless switching,
both policies restore availability (Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}(b)): no admissible
alternate was ever missing.
Throughput is different (Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}(a)).
Event-avoiding handoff, the policy that clears every in-line event, still
exceeds the limit as deployed, though narrowly (\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrCi\%, with
\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrOver\ of \DepGenTwoReps\ station-replications over it), whereas
rate-aware handoff meets it
(\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrCi\%, none over). The reason is
the cost of the escape route. During events the interference is moderate: the
median $I/N$ of Starlink's serving beam is $\DepInServMedDb$~dB against a clear-sky
C/N of \DepCnMedMinDb--\DepCnMedMaxDb~dB, so staying loses little, whereas the
admissible alternate is a lower, more distant OneWeb satellite with a weaker
link. Event-avoiding handoff uses an alternate with a weaker desired signal
\DepGenTwoEvAvAltPct\% of the time, even when its SINR is lower than that of the
interfered link; rate-aware handoff uses an alternate only
\DepGenTwoRateMaxAltPct\% of the time, and only when its SINR is higher. Because event-avoiding handoff lies close to the limit, the side on
which it falls depends on the scenario: as filed it meets the limit
(\FilGenTwoEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\FilGenTwoEvAvDegrCi\%), and so it does with the
unambiguous 43$^\circ$ Gen2 shell alone
(\FortyThreeOnlyEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\FortyThreeOnlyEvAvDegrCi\%) and against Gen1 alone,
for which the protection limits do not apply (\DepGenOneEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenOneEvAvDegrCi\%),
whereas against all Starlink it exceeds
(\DepGenAllEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenAllEvAvDegrCi\%). What is robust is the gap: in
every case, as filed and as deployed, rate-aware handoff stays clearly below
the limit (largest \FilGenAllRateMaxDegrPct$\pm$\FilGenAllRateMaxDegrCi\%, all
Starlink as filed), and without interruption a \TsCheckStepS~s step on
\TsCheckDays\ days changes no verdict, although it yields more switches.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Four implications follow for compatibility showings.
\emph{The assumed selection strategy decides the verdict.} The FCC lets an
analysis assume the incumbent's satellite selection strategy~\cite{fcc24117}. At
the same interferer power, our primary verdict flips between ``exceeded'' and ``met''
depending on that assumption alone, and on the switching it implies (dwell time
and interruption), so showings should state and justify both, and regulators
should test more than one policy. \emph{Avoiding the trigger is not the same as
protecting the link.} The in-line event triggers same-round
band splitting, not the inter-round protection test. In the scenarios we tested,
a victim that clears every in-line event loses more throughput under the
protection limits than one that tolerates moderate interference. A OneWeb
terminal faces both regimes at once, band splitting toward Starlink Gen1 and the
protection limits toward Gen2, and after the ten-year sunset the Gen2 relationship
also moves to band splitting. Whether a combination of
satellite selection, band splitting, and coordination serves both well requires
modeling the two regimes jointly, which we leave open. \emph{Switching is the price of protection.} Relative to no interference-driven
handoff, rate-aware handoff lowers the degradation from \DepGenTwoNoHoDegrPct\% to
\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrPct\% at \DepDwellRateMaxNTenSwitchesPerHour\ instead of
\IntRateMaxBaseSwitchesPerHour\ switches per hour, and it dominates event-avoiding
handoff, which loses more throughput with more switches
(\DepDwellEvAvNTenSwitchesPerHour\ per hour). Whether this is affordable depends on the
interruption each switch causes. \emph{Measure the criterion, not a proxy:} only a screen at $\theta^{*}$ matches
the $\Delta T/T$ criterion. Unlike entrant-side
selection~\cite{kim2026selection}, the response that decides compliance here is
the protected system's own.

\section{Limitations}
(a) Propagation is clear-sky and the rate map is a margin-reduced Shannon bound;
with rain fade and discrete modulation the availability gate and $\Delta U_g$ would
be evaluated nearer to threshold. (b) The S.1428 dish pattern approximates flat
panels, which also lose gain when scanning toward low elevation. (c) Non-serving interferers are nadir-pointing. (d) The
baseline uses the highest-elevation satellite and handoff ignores satellite
capacity. (e) Only the user downlink is modeled. (f) The OneWeb EIRP is taken
from~\cite{delportillo2019}; its constant EIRP from fixed beams and the
\StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ mask are assumptions. (g) The equal Gen2 shells are an assumption. (h) Interruption is added after simulation and does not enter the handoff
decision, so an interruption-aware policy would switch less; a \TsCheckStepS~s step
also yields more switches than the \TimeStepS~s step (\TsTwoEvAvSwitchesPerHour\ vs.\
\TsTenEvAvSwitchesPerHour\ per hour event-avoiding, \TsTwoRateMaxSwitchesPerHour\ vs.\
\TsTenRateMaxSwitchesPerHour\ rate-aware), so the tolerable interruption is likely
smaller than stated. (i) $J_2$ propagation drifts from SGP4 after the first days,
so later days act as further phasings of the observed shells (the statistics we
use show no trend); as-deployed intervals describe day-to-day variation within
one snapshot, and as-filed intervals only the sampled phasings. All statements hold under
the modeled assumptions.

\section{Conclusion}
The FCC's NGSO sharing rule promises an incumbent that an entrant will cost it at
most \DegradeLimitPct\% of throughput and \UnavailLimitPct\% of availability. For
OneWeb and Starlink Gen2, whether a compatibility analysis keeps this promise
depends on an assumption the rule leaves open: how the incumbent's users switch
satellites.
Without interference-driven handoff, the modeled compatibility assessment with
Starlink at its filed maximum exceeds both limits. With a 25$^\circ$ mask and unrestricted, lossless switching, a victim that
dodges every in-line event moves to weaker links and, as deployed, narrowly
exceeds the throughput limit, whereas one that keeps an interfered link while it
is still the better one meets both limits; this gap persists in every geometry
we tested. The better policy has limits
too: among the tested settings, a \DepDwellRateMaxDegrFirstExceedS~s dwell time
breaks its throughput limit, and a \DepDwellRateMaxUnavFirstExceedS~s dwell, a
\OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ mask breaks its availability limit, and beyond about
\IntRateMaxMaxTauUnavS~s of interruption per extra switch our upper-bound,
step-sensitive estimate no longer guarantees it. Because the FCC lets compatibility analyses assume the
incumbent's selection strategy, showings should state that policy and its
switching constraints, test more than one, and evaluate the $\Delta T/T$
criterion itself rather than a geometric screen. Future work will add rain fade
and gateway links.
