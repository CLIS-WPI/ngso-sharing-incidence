\begin{abstract}
Switching serving satellites to avoid interference can sacrifice
desired-signal strength and throughput. This raises a question for co-frequency
low-Earth-orbit systems: can the protected system's satellite selection policy
change whether an interference assessment meets the FCC's protection limits? We
quantify this dependence for Starlink Gen2 and OneWeb under 47 CFR~25.261, in filed geometry and
in a catalog snapshot of the deployed satellites. For every candidate satellite
we recompute the desired signal and the aggregate interference, and we compare
no handoff, event-avoiding handoff, and rate-maximizing handoff against one
interference-free baseline. In clear sky, with Starlink at its filed maximum
PFD, a 25$^\circ$ mask, and unrestricted switching, the mean worst-station throughput
degradation across replications, as deployed, is \DepGenTwoNoHoDegrPct\% without handoff,
\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrPct\% with event-avoiding handoff, and
\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrPct\% with rate-maximizing handoff, against a
\DegradeLimitPct\% limit. The policy alone thus moves the result across the
limit in this scenario, narrowly for event-avoiding handoff: avoiding every
in-line event selects weaker desired links, whereas rate-maximizing handoff
keeps the interfered link when it is still the better one. In separate sensitivity tests, an upper-bound estimate of the added
unavailability stays within the limit only for subsecond interruptions per extra
switch, and a 60~s minimum dwell time or a 55$^\circ$ mask breaks it for both. Because the
FCC lets compatibility analyses assume the incumbent's selection strategy, that
assumption, and the switching it implies, should be stated and tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Ku-band sky over a user terminal is now crowded: our snapshot counts
\NStarlinkOperational\ operational Starlink satellites, \NStarlinkGenTwo\ of them
second-generation, and \NOnewebOp\ OneWeb satellites, all transmitting to users
in the same band. Their satellites keep crossing each other's lines of
sight to users, and someone has to give way.

In the United States, who gives way is written into 47 CFR~25.261~\cite{cfr25261},
the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rule for sharing spectrum among
non-geostationary-orbit (NGSO) satellite systems. The FCC licenses such systems
in batches called \emph{processing rounds}; a system licensed in an earlier round
is the \emph{incumbent} and one licensed in a later round is the \emph{entrant}.
Two systems are \emph{co-frequency} when they are authorized to use the same
frequencies for the same type of link. When interference raises a receiver's
noise temperature by more than \DttThresholdPct\% ($\Delta T/T$), an
\emph{in-line event} occurs; absent coordination, systems of the \emph{same}
round then split the band for its duration (\S\,25.261(c))~\cite{cfr25261,fcc2329}.
Between rounds a different mechanism applies: before operating, an entrant must
coordinate with each earlier-round incumbent or submit a \emph{compatibility
showing}, based on a degraded-throughput methodology, that it causes the
incumbent no more than \DegradeLimitPct\% time-weighted throughput degradation
and no more than \UnavailLimitPct\% absolute change in availability, on links
with at least \RuleBaselineAvailPct\% baseline availability (the fraction of time
the link works without interference) at a carrier-to-noise ratio (C/N) of
\CnGateDb~dB (\S\,25.261(d))~\cite{cfr25261,fcc24117}. Ten years after the
first grant in the later round, this protection ends and (c) applies across the
rounds (\S\,25.261(e)). OneWeb~\cite{fcc1777} and the first generation of
Starlink (Gen1)~\cite{fcc1838} were licensed in the 2016 round and Starlink's
second generation (Gen2)~\cite{fcc2291} in the 2020 round, so the rule promises
OneWeb protection against Starlink Gen2. A separate 2026 order rewrote sharing
between NGSO and geostationary systems~\cite{fcc2626} but left these criteria
unchanged.

The FCC evaluates the limits through compatibility analyses and allows such an
analysis to use an \emph{assumed} satellite selection strategy for the incumbent
when the actual one is not provided~\cite{fcc24117}. How much that assumption
matters has not been measured. Interference between the OneWeb and SpaceX user
downlinks was anticipated early~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{delportillo2019}; later work
asked whether interference among dense NGSO constellations matters in
practice~\cite{braun2019} and designed entrant-side satellite selection for
in-band coexistence in Ka band~\cite{kim2025,kim2026selection}. None of these
evaluates the FCC's own criteria, and none asks what the \emph{victim's} handoff
does to the verdict.

This paper does.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CLIS-WPI/ngso-sharing-incidence}}
We model the constellations both \emph{as filed} (the orbits in the operators'
FCC filings) and \emph{as deployed} (the satellites in orbit today), apply the
rule's exact criteria at every latitude, and evaluate the victim with and
without handoff to another of its own satellites. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A general model of NGSO co-frequency interference, of the \S\,25.261
criteria, and of victim handoff policies, instantiated for Starlink Gen2 and
OneWeb, the only co-frequency user-downlink pair among Starlink, OneWeb, and
Amazon Leo (formerly Project Kuiper).
\item To our knowledge, the first latitude-resolved measurement of how often the
\S\,25.261 event fires and whether the \S\,25.261(d) limits hold, as filed and as
deployed, at two sourced Starlink power levels.
\item The finding that the verdict depends on the victim's handoff policy and
its switching: as deployed, leaving every interfered satellite narrowly exceeds
the throughput limit, whereas staying when the interfered link is still the better
one meets it; availability, in turn, needs frequent, near-lossless switching
and a low elevation mask; we map where each policy passes and fails.
\item An analytic approximation that turns the regulatory power criterion into a
geometric one through an equivalent off-axis angle, independent of terminal size.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\emph{Systems and geometry.} We consider a set $\mathcal{K}$ of NGSO systems.
System $k \in \mathcal{K}$ consists of shells $\mathcal{S}_k$ (altitude,
inclination, planes, satellites per plane) and is authorized in a processing
round $\rho_k$. Satellite positions evolve over discrete time
$t \in \mathcal{T}$, and earth stations $g \in \mathcal{G}$ are placed at known
geodetic positions. Satellite $j$ is visible from $g$ when its elevation is at
least the minimum elevation $\epsilon_k^{\min}$ of its system;
$\mathcal{V}_{k}(g,t)$ denotes the visible satellites of system $k$.

\emph{Co-frequency pairs.} Each system holds authorized frequency ranges
$\mathcal{F}_k^{\ell}$ for each link type $\ell$ (user/gateway, up/down). An
ordered pair $(A,B)$ is screened on link type $\ell$ only if
$\mathcal{F}_A^{\ell} \cap \mathcal{F}_B^{\ell} \neq \emptyset$; the coupling is
evaluated at the centre frequency $f$ of the overlap, with wavelength $\lambda$.

\emph{Signal, interference, and the in-line event.} A victim station $g$ of
system $A$ served by satellite $s \in \mathcal{V}_A(g,t)$ has carrier-to-noise
ratio and aggregate interference from system $B$
\begin{gather}
c_g(t;s) = \frac{\mathrm{EIRP}_s\, G_{\text{rx}}(0)}{L(r_{g,s})\,N}, \label{eq:cn}\\
I_g(t;s) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}_B(g,t)} \frac{\mathrm{EIRP}_j\,
g_j(\phi_{g,j})\, G_{\text{rx}}(\theta_{g,j}^{s})}{L(r_{g,j})},
\label{eq:agg}
\end{gather}
where $\mathrm{EIRP}$ is the peak equivalent isotropically radiated power density
(linear), $g_j(\phi)$ the normalized transmit pattern of satellite $j$ at the
off-axis angle $\phi_{g,j}$ toward $g$, $G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ the victim
receive gain at the angle $\theta_{g,j}^{s}$ between $s$ and $j$ seen from $g$
(the terminal points at $s$), $L(r)=(4\pi r/\lambda)^2$ the free-space loss at
slant range $r$, and $N = kT_{\text{sys}}$ the noise density. The in-line event
on link $s$ is
\begin{equation}
E_g(t;s) = \mathbb{1}\{\Delta T/T = I_g(t;s)/N > \delta\},
\label{eq:event}
\end{equation}
with $\delta$ the regulatory threshold. Three interferer sets bracket the unknown
beam plans of system $B$: \emph{realistic}, where $B$'s serving satellite for the
site radiates its main beam ($g_j = 1$) and every other visible $B$ satellite
contributes through its sidelobe, with its boresight approximated by nadir;
\emph{co-located}, where only $B$'s serving satellite is counted; and an
\emph{upper bound}, where every visible $B$ satellite radiates at peak toward $g$.

\emph{Interferer power models.} Under \emph{constant EIRP}, $\mathrm{EIRP}_j$ is
fixed. Under \emph{constant PFD}, the operator holds the surface power-flux
density (PFD) at $\mathrm{PFD}_0$ while steering, so that (in dB)
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EIRP}_{j,\text{dB}}(r) = \mathrm{PFD}_{0,\text{dB}} + 10\log_{10}(4\pi r^2),
\label{eq:pfd}
\end{equation}
capped at its value at the range $r_{\max}$ of the minimum service elevation.

\emph{Handoff policies.} The baseline policy serves $g$ from the highest-elevation
visible satellite $s_0(g,t)$. A handoff policy considers switching only while the
baseline link is in event, $E_g(t;s_0)=1$, and only to \emph{admissible}
alternates $j \in \mathcal{V}_A(g,t)\setminus\{s_0\}$ with $E_g(t;j)=0$, each
evaluated with its own link budget (\ref{eq:cn})--(\ref{eq:agg}). With
$\gamma_g(t;s) = c_g(t;s)/(1+I_g(t;s)/N)$ the SINR,
\emph{event-avoiding} handoff always leaves $s_0$ for the admissible alternate
with the highest $\gamma$, whereas \emph{rate-maximizing} handoff picks the higher $\gamma$ among
the admissible alternates and the degraded link $s_0$, i.e., it maximizes the rate
over this restricted set only. If no alternate
is admissible, the event is \emph{unresolvable} and the victim stays on $s_0$.
Two constraints restrict switching: only the $K$ highest-elevation satellites
are candidates, and the victim keeps a satellite for a minimum dwell time of $N_{\text{s}}$
seconds after a switch. A handoff interruption of $\tau$ seconds is added after
simulation: with $E$ the excess of the policy's switches per hour over the
baseline's, the interrupted time fraction is $f = E\tau/3600$, and
$\Delta U_{g,\tau} = \Delta U_g + f$ and $D_{g,\tau} = 1-(1-D_g)(1-f)$, per station
and day, using the policy's time-averaged rate; overlap with existing outages is
not removed, so $\Delta U_{g,\tau}$ is an upper bound.

\emph{Protection metrics.} Let $s(t)$ be the satellite actually used. With the
rate map $R(\gamma) = \log_2(1+\gamma/m)$ for a served instant and $R=0$ for an
unserved one ($m$ an implementation margin) and a C/N threshold $\gamma_0$,
\begin{gather}
D_g = 1 - \frac{\overline{R(\gamma_g(t;s(t)))}}{\overline{R(c_g(t;s_0))}},\\
\Delta U_g = \Pr\{\gamma_g(t;s(t)) < \gamma_0\} - \Pr\{c_g(t;s_0) < \gamma_0\},
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{gather}
over $t\in\mathcal{T}$, with an unserved instant counted as unavailable. The
baseline is the interference-free link under the baseline policy. A link
qualifies for protection only if its baseline availability
$\Pr\{c_g \ge \gamma_0\}$ is at least $a_{\min}$, and the rule is met when
$D_g \le D_{\max}$ and $\Delta U_g \le U_{\max}$. Both metrics are invariant to the
channel bandwidth.

\section{Regulatory Instantiation and Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\emph{Rule parameters.} Under 47 CFR~25.261, $\delta = \DttThreshold$
($I/N > \InThresholdDb$~dB)~\cite{cfr25261}, and for \S\,25.261(d)
$D_{\max} = \DegradeLimitPct\%$, $U_{\max} = \UnavailLimitPct\%$,
$\gamma_0 = \CnGateDb$~dB, and $a_{\min} = \RuleBaselineAvailPct\%$~\cite{fcc24117};
\S\,25.261(d) is evaluated only where it legally applies, i.e., OneWeb victim and
Starlink Gen2 interferer. Our evaluation is a compatibility analysis under the
modeled assumptions, of the kind a showing under (d) contains and with which an
entrant's operations must then conform (\S\,25.261(d)(4)). It does not represent
Starlink's actual beam scheduling, operating power, or coordination agreements,
so an exceeded limit means a failed analysis under these assumptions, not an
observed violation.

\emph{Systems.} Screening the authorized bands of Starlink, OneWeb, and Amazon Leo
(formerly Project Kuiper; ``Kuiper'' in its filings) leaves one co-frequency
user-downlink pair, Starlink and OneWeb in \SharedBandLoGhz--\SharedBandHiGhz~GHz,
coupled at $f = \SharedFreqGhz$~GHz; Kuiper's user downlink is in Ka band.
\emph{As filed}, Starlink Gen2 is the three shells granted in FCC 22-91 at
53, 43, and 33$^\circ$ inclination~\cite{fcc2291}, modeled as
Walker-Delta shells of equal size (\FilGenTwoNSats\ satellites in total, a
Walker approximation of the \num{7500}-satellite grant; an assumption) propagated with a two-body model plus $J_2$ drift over
\EventNPhasings\ independent phasings (random rotations of each constellation).
\emph{As deployed}, positions come from a CelesTrak orbital-element
snapshot~\cite{celestrak} of \SnapshotDate\ (\NStarlinkRaw\ Starlink and
\NOnewebRaw\ OneWeb objects; \NStarlinkOperational\ and \NOnewebOp\ at
\OpAltStarlinkLoKm--\OpAltStarlinkHiKm\ and \OpAltOnewebLoKm--\OpAltOnewebHiKm~km).
Each Starlink satellite is assigned to Gen2 or Gen1 by shell: the
\NSatsFortyThree\ satellites at 43$^\circ$ are Gen2 (no Gen1 shell exists
there), the \NSatsFiftyThreeGenTwo\ at 53$^\circ$ with an international designator
(COSPAR launch) of \GenTwoLaunchCutoff\ or later, the first launch into the
Gen2-only 43$^\circ$ shell after the Gen2 grant, are Gen2 (an assumption: a later
replacement launched into a Gen1 shell would be misassigned), and the rest are Gen1,
giving \NStarlinkGenTwo\ Gen2 and \NStarlinkGenOne\ Gen1 satellites; the
33$^\circ$ shell is still empty. Operational altitudes
(\AltFortyThreeMedKm~km at 43$^\circ$, \AltFiftyThreeGenTwoMedKm~km at
53$^\circ$) differ from those filed, so we classify by inclination and
launch rather than altitude. Each run covers \DurationH~h in \TimeStepS~s steps
from \NStartTimes\ start times \StartSpacingH~h apart (non-overlapping); the
as-deployed satellites are propagated from the snapshot with the same $J_2$ model. The \NStations\ stations
lie at latitudes \LatMinDeg--\LatMaxDeg$^\circ$ in \LatStepDeg$^\circ$ steps on
\NLongitudes\ meridians (0, 90, 180, and 270$^\circ$\,E). The victim gain follows the International
Telecommunication Union (ITU-R) earth-station pattern S.1428~\cite{itu1428} and
the interferer the ITU-R satellite pattern S.1528~\cite{itu1528};
$m = 10^{\ImplMarginDb/10}$ (a \ImplMarginDb~dB margin). Propagation is clear-sky free space; rain fade, which the
FCC assesses case by case~\cite{fcc24117}, is not modeled. Table~\ref{tab:params}
lists the parameters and their sources.

\emph{Interferer power.} Starlink Ku holds a constant surface PFD while
steering~\cite{fcc2291}, so (\ref{eq:pfd}) applies with two sourced anchors at
the \PfdAnchorElevDeg$^\circ$ service edge (the lowest elevation at which Starlink
serves users). (a) \emph{Gen2 filed maximum} (primary): the 47 CFR~25.208(b)
limit, $\PfdLimitDbw$~dBW/m$^2$/MHz~\cite{cfr25208}; the top of the filed Gen2
EIRP density~\cite{fcc2291}, \StarlinkFiledTopDbw~dBW/MHz after conversion from
the per-4-kHz bandwidth in which FCC filings state Ku-band densities, gives
$\FiledPfdDbw$, within \FiledPfdOffsetDb~dB. (b) \emph{Gen1 design point}: the
constant PFD of the 2016 filing~\cite[Table~A.7-1]{spacex2016},
$\GenOnePfdDbw$~dBW/m$^2$/MHz, \PfdOffsetGenOneDb~dB lower, used as a sourced
lower operating point. Section~25.208(b) covers only
\PfdScopeLoGhz--\PfdScopeHiGhz~GHz of the shared band. OneWeb transmits at
constant EIRP from fixed beams.

\emph{Aggregation.} Every \S\,25.261(d) number is the worst qualifying station per
replication (phasing or start time), averaged across replications, with a
\CiPct\% $t$-interval; a limit is \emph{exceeded} only if the whole interval lies
above it and \emph{met} only if it lies below. We also report how many of the
\FilGenTwoReps\ station-replications exceed a limit.

\section{Analytic Approximation}
\label{sec:analytic}
Why should a power criterion depend on geometry at all? Under constant PFD
(\ref{eq:pfd}), the main-beam interference
$\mathrm{PFD}_0\,\lambda^2/(4\pi)\,G_{\text{rx}}(\theta)$ is range-independent,
so in the co-located case the event (\ref{eq:event}) depends only on the victim
off-axis angle $\theta$ and reduces to $\theta < \theta^{*}$, where, with $N$
and $\mathrm{PFD}_0$ in matching per-MHz units,
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{rx}}(\theta^{*}) = \delta_{\text{dB}} + N_{\text{dB}} -
\mathrm{PFD}_{0,\text{dB}} - 10\log_{10}(\lambda^2/4\pi).
\label{eq:thetastar}
\end{equation}
Whenever $\theta^{*}$ falls in the sidelobe envelope of the receive pattern, which
for S.1428 is $G = 32 - 25\log_{10}\theta$ ($1^\circ$--$48^\circ$), the aperture
cancels: for OneWeb, $\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$ (spread
\AnalyticThetaSpreadDeg$^\circ$ across \EirpHeadDiam--\AperMaxM~m), which explains
why the event fraction barely depends on terminal size. The co-located event
probability is then
\begin{equation}
P = v_A v_B\,\Pr\{\angle(\hat u_A,\hat u_B) < \theta^{*}\},
\label{eq:pevent}
\end{equation}
where $v_k = 1 - e^{-n_k}$ is the probability that system $k$ has a satellite in
view, $n_k$ its mean visible count from the Walker latitude density averaged over
the argument of latitude, and each serving direction $\hat u_k$ is the nearest of
$n_k$ satellites uniform in the ground footprint, mapped to the look angle.
Feeding the simulated marginals into the separation integral reproduces the
simulated separation probability within \DiagnosisIndepMaxErrPct\ percentage
points (pp), supporting the approximation that the two serving directions are
independent. On the filed Gen2 shells, (\ref{eq:pevent}) is within
$\AnalyticSignedMinInclPct$ to \AnalyticSignedMaxInclPct~pp of the simulation up to
\AnalyticInclLatDeg$^\circ$ latitude; beyond the Gen2 inclinations, where the
satellite density falls to zero, the uniform-footprint assumption fails and the
error reaches \AnalyticMaxAbsBeyondPct~pp. Equation (\ref{eq:pevent}) therefore
gives only the order of magnitude of the event fraction; its main use is (\ref{eq:thetastar}), which is exact under constant
PFD, and the simulation remains the primary result.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
We follow one OneWeb user through the rule, from the trigger to the protection
limits, with Starlink Gen2 at its filed maximum unless stated otherwise.

\emph{The trigger fires often, and mostly on OneWeb.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:events} shows the fraction of time with $\Delta T/T$ above the
threshold. For a OneWeb user it peaks at \BindDepWorstPct$\pm$\BindDepWorstCi\% at
\BindDepWorstLat$^\circ$ as deployed (\BindFilWorstPct\% as filed), whereas a
Starlink user peaks at \BindDepStarWorstPct\%: OneWeb carries
\BurdenRatioDep$\times$ the exposure. As victim, OneWeb at \OnewebAltKm~km looks
through the PFD-limited beams of the lower, denser Starlink shells; as interferer,
its longer slant range and lower EIRP density weaken its contribution. The event fraction follows Gen2 visibility, which is highest at
mid-latitudes (\VisDepLatForty\ satellites above the mask at 40$^\circ$) and collapses
beyond the 53$^\circ$ shell inclination (\VisDepLatSixty\ at
60$^\circ$); at low latitudes the as-deployed fraction exceeds the as-filed
one although fewer Starlink satellites are visible (\VisDepLatZero\ versus
\VisFilLatZero\ at the equator), so the event rate is not set by the interferer
count alone.

\emph{A narrow geometric screen would miss most of it.}
A common shortcut declares an in-line event only when the two serving satellites
are nearly aligned. With a \ThetaScreenDeg$^\circ$ separation threshold, that
screen fires only \ThetaColoPeakPct\% of the time at its peak (co-located,
Gen2 as deployed). Equation
(\ref{eq:thetastar}) explains the gap: Starlink's serving beam pushes OneWeb's
$\Delta T/T$ over the threshold through the terminal's sidelobes out to
$\theta^{*} = \AnalyticThetaStarDeg^\circ$.

\emph{Without handoff, the showing fails.}
If the OneWeb user makes no interference-driven handoff (it still follows the
baseline policy's own changes of highest-elevation satellite), both limits are broken in both
geometries (Table~\ref{tab:handoff}): throughput degrades by
\DepGenTwoNoHoDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoNoHoDegrCi\% as deployed
(\DepGenTwoNoHoDegrOver\ of \DepGenTwoReps\ station-replications over the limit)
and availability drops by \DepGenTwoNoHoUnavPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoNoHoUnavCi\%. All
OneWeb links qualify (\QualOneStationReps\ of \QualOneStationRepsTotal). The
availability limit is the harder one: at \PfdOffsetThreeDb~dB below the filed
maximum the throughput loss meets its limit
(\TabPfdMinusThreeDegrNoDivPct$\pm$\TabPfdMinusThreeDegrNoDivCi\%), whereas the
availability change (\TabPfdMinusThreeUnavNoDivPct$\pm$\TabPfdMinusThreeUnavNoDivCi\%)
still exceeds its limit; only at the Gen1 design PFD are both met
(\TabGenOneDegrNoDivPct$\pm$\TabGenOneDegrNoDivCi\% and
\TabGenOneUnavNoDivPct$\pm$\TabGenOneUnavNoDivCi\%; Table~\ref{tab:cases}).
A Starlink user, for comparison only, loses
\DegrDepStarPct$\pm$\DegrDepStarCi\% of throughput.

\emph{Which handoff decides throughput.}
Now let the OneWeb user switch satellites during events (Table~\ref{tab:handoff},
Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}). All three policies share one run, one interference-free
baseline, and a full link budget for every candidate, so differences come from
the policy alone. With a 25$^\circ$ mask and unrestricted, lossless switching,
both policies restore availability: no admissible alternate was ever missing.
Throughput is different.
Event-avoiding handoff, the policy that clears every in-line event, still
exceeds the limit as deployed, though narrowly (\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrCi\%, with
\DepGenTwoEvAvDegrOver\ of \DepGenTwoReps\ station-replications over it), whereas
rate-maximizing handoff meets it
(\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenTwoRateMaxDegrCi\%, none over). The reason is
the cost of the escape route. During events the interference is moderate: the
median $I/N$ of Starlink's serving beam is $\DepInServMedDb$~dB against a clear-sky
C/N of \DepCnMedMinDb--\DepCnMedMaxDb~dB, so staying loses little, whereas the
admissible alternate is a lower, more distant OneWeb satellite with a weaker
link. Event-avoiding handoff uses an alternate with a weaker desired signal
\DepGenTwoEvAvAltPct\% of the time, even when its SINR is lower than that of the
interfered link; rate-maximizing handoff uses an alternate only
\DepGenTwoRateMaxAltPct\% of the time, and only when its SINR is higher. Because event-avoiding handoff lies close to the limit, the side on
which it falls depends on the scenario: as filed it meets the limit
(\FilGenTwoEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\FilGenTwoEvAvDegrCi\%), and so it does with the
unambiguous 43$^\circ$ Gen2 shell alone
(\FortyThreeOnlyEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\FortyThreeOnlyEvAvDegrCi\%) and against Gen1 alone,
for which (d) does not apply (\DepGenOneEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenOneEvAvDegrCi\%),
whereas against all Starlink it exceeds
(\DepGenAllEvAvDegrPct$\pm$\DepGenAllEvAvDegrCi\%). What is robust is the gap: in
every case, as filed and as deployed, rate-maximizing handoff stays clearly below
the limit (largest \FilGenAllRateMaxDegrPct$\pm$\FilGenAllRateMaxDegrCi\%, all
Starlink as filed), and without interruption a \TsCheckStepS~s step on
\TsCheckDays\ days changes no verdict, although it yields more switches.

\emph{Where each policy passes and fails.} Table~\ref{tab:handoff} maps the
boundary. Limiting candidates to the two highest-elevation satellites leaves
events unresolved about \DepKTwoUnresPct\% of the time, but both policies keep
their verdicts. A minimum dwell time per satellite raises both policies'
losses; rate-maximizing throughput exceeds the
limit first at \DepDwellRateMaxDegrFirstExceedS~s among the tested dwell times, and the availability
limit is first exceeded at \DepDwellEvAvUnavFirstExceedS~s (event-avoiding) and
\DepDwellRateMaxUnavFirstExceedS~s (rate-maximizing). Without a dwell
constraint, event-avoiding handoff switches \DepDwellEvAvNTenSwitchesPerHour\
and rate-maximizing handoff \DepDwellRateMaxNTenSwitchesPerHour\ times per hour,
against \IntRateMaxBaseSwitchesPerHour\ for the baseline. If each extra switch
interrupts the link for $\tau$ seconds, the post-hoc upper-bound estimate of the
availability change stays within the limit up to approximately
\IntEvAvMaxTauUnavS~s (event-avoiding) and \IntRateMaxMaxTauUnavS~s
(rate-maximizing), and rate-maximizing throughput up to \IntRateMaxMaxTauDegrS~s.
OneWeb's filed design keeps a satellite above \OnewebMinElevSensDeg$^\circ$ for its
users~\cite[Sec.~2.2]{delportillo2019}. Under that mask only the stations at
\MinElevQualLatLo--\MinElevQualLatHi$^\circ$ (\MinElevQualStations\ of \NStations)
reach the baseline availability needed for protection, so below
\MinElevQualLatLo$^\circ$ \S\,25.261(d) is not even defined; where it is, only
\MinElevNAboveMin--\MinElevNAboveMax\ OneWeb satellites are above the mask on
average, and both policies exceed the availability limit, while their throughput
loss stays within its limit (Table~\ref{tab:handoff}). This matches
the observation that OneWeb's fixed, non-steerable beams leave it only a passive
role in in-line coordination~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{delportillo2019}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Three implications follow for compatibility showings under \S\,25.261(d).
\emph{The assumed selection strategy decides the verdict.} The FCC lets an
analysis assume the incumbent's satellite selection strategy~\cite{fcc24117}. At
the same interferer power, our primary verdict flips between ``exceeded'' and ``met''
depending on that assumption alone, and on the switching it implies (dwell time
and interruption), so showings should state and justify both, and regulators
should test more than one policy. \emph{Avoiding the trigger is not the same as
protecting the link.} The in-line event triggers the same-round
band-splitting procedure (c), not the inter-round test (d). In the scenarios we
tested, a victim that clears every in-line event gives up more throughput under
(d) than one that tolerates moderate interference. A OneWeb terminal faces both
relationships at once, (c) toward Starlink Gen1 and (d) toward Gen2, and after the
sunset of (d) the Gen2 relationship also moves to (c). Whether a combination of
satellite selection, band splitting, and coordination serves both well requires
modeling the two regimes jointly, which we leave open. Scoring the alternate with the serving satellite's C/N, a natural
shortcut, hides this cost (\DepGenTwoLegacyDegrPct\% degradation). \emph{Measure the criterion, not a proxy.}
Geometric in-line screens understate how often the $\Delta T/T$ criterion applies,
because the trigger reaches deep into the victim terminal's sidelobes. Unlike
entrant-side satellite selection for coexistence~\cite{kim2026selection}, which
designs the interferer's response, our results show that the protected system's
own response, which the entrant cannot control, determines compliance.

\section{Limitations}
(a) Propagation is clear-sky and the rate map is a margin-reduced Shannon bound;
with rain fade and discrete modulation the availability gate and $\Delta U_g$ would
be evaluated nearer to threshold. (b) The S.1428 dish pattern approximates flat
panels, which also lose gain when scanning toward low elevation. (c) Non-serving interferers are nadir-pointing. (d) The
baseline uses the highest-elevation satellite and handoff ignores satellite
capacity. (e) Only the user downlink is modeled. (f) The OneWeb EIRP is taken
from~\cite{delportillo2019}; its constant EIRP from fixed beams and the
\StationMinElevDeg$^\circ$ mask are assumptions. (g) The equal split of the Gen2
grant and the launch-designator classification at 53$^\circ$ are assumptions. (h) Handoff is modeled at the \TimeStepS~s step; interruption is added afterwards
per extra switch, and a \TsCheckStepS~s step yields more switches per hour
(\TsTwoEvAvSwitchesPerHour\ versus \TsTenEvAvSwitchesPerHour\ for event-avoiding),
so the tolerable interruption is likely smaller than stated. (i) Without drag, $J_2$ propagation drifts from SGP4
for individual Starlink satellites after the first days, mostly along track, so
later days act as further phasings of the observed shells rather than forecasts;
the visible-count and event statistics we use show no trend across days.
As-deployed intervals describe day-to-day variation conditional on one snapshot;
tests found no detectable dependence between days, but with little power.
As-filed intervals cover only the sampled phasings. All statements hold under
the modeled assumptions.

\section{Conclusion}
The FCC's NGSO sharing rule promises an incumbent that an entrant will cost it at
most \DegradeLimitPct\% of throughput and \UnavailLimitPct\% of availability. For
OneWeb and Starlink Gen2 in the Ku user downlink, the rule's trigger fires up to
\BindDepWorstPct$\pm$\BindDepWorstCi\% of the time for a OneWeb user, mostly on
the protected system. In a compatibility analysis without handoff, Starlink at its filed
maximum exceeds both limits; only its lower Gen1 design power meets them. As deployed, with a 25$^\circ$ mask and unrestricted, lossless switching, a victim
that dodges every in-line event narrowly exceeds the throughput limit, whereas
one that stays on an interfered satellite whenever that link is still the better
one meets both limits. In separate sensitivity tests, the latter
policy meets both limits at the shorter tested dwell times but exceeds the
throughput limit first at \DepDwellRateMaxDegrFirstExceedS~s among the tested dwell times; under unrestricted
switching, the post-hoc upper-bound estimate keeps availability within its limit up to
about \IntRateMaxMaxTauUnavS~s of interruption per extra switch, a model estimate
that is sensitive to the time step. Whether
\S\,25.261(d) protects an incumbent therefore depends on its handoff policy and
switching, which compatibility analyses may only assume. Future work will add rain fade and gateway links.
